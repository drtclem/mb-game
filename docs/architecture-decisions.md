# MB Game: Architecture Decisions

North star: keep it cheap, and keep the option to put it online for people to play or sell it later (itch.io first, Steam and app stores possible).

**Current target: Game Week.** Due Thursday at midnight, played and judged Friday morning. We're entering under **Option 3: Multiplayer Racing**, as a competitive two-player marching game. Section 0 maps the brief to the plan; sections marked **(Game Week)** changed for it.

## 0. Game Week brief → our plan

**Pitch:** two friends march on neighboring dots in the same band, each on their own device. Same show, same music, head-to-head. The recap sheet decides who won. Your friend's drift moves the form you're scored against, so you compete *and* depend on each other.

| Brief requirement | How we meet it |
|---|---|
| At least two people racing together, clear way to join | Online peer-to-peer, room code or invite link (#14) |
| At least two distinct tracks | At least **two shows**, each with its own music, drill, tempo and field look (#15) |
| Complete flow: start, race, finish, results, play again | Lobby → calibration → count-off → show → side-by-side recap → **Rematch** / **Next show** |
| Clear feedback on position and who won | Both players' live DOT / FORM on the HUD, both players marked on the corner map, winner banner on the recap |
| A mechanic beyond basic steering | **Judges and staff** walking through the band (block your view, collisions), plus **big moments** that score crowd points |
| Multiplayer reliable enough to finish a race | Each device judges its own player, so lag never touches scoring; relay fallback for blocked networks; graceful drop handling (#14) |

**Bars every game must clear, and what they mean for us:**
- **Polished, cohesive visual style (pass/fail).** A stylized-realistic look, applied consistently everywhere (#10).
- **All assets generated for the game, no pre-made assets.** No asset-store or library characters, sample packs or free crowd sounds. Music is AI-generated for the game (#9); models and art are built or generated by us (#10).
- **Playable by a new player without help.** A short how-to-play screen, plus a guided first run (#16).
- **Browser link, no install.** Already the plan (#1).
- **Polish over size.** Two finished shows beat four rough ones.

**To ask the instructor up front:**
- Does a competitive marching game count as "Racing"?
- Can we use **AI-generated music** (styled from our own arrangement of public-domain pieces)?
- Can we use **AI-generated 3D models** (for the two player marchers)?
- Do voice lines we record ourselves count?

## Locked

### 1. Platform
- Browser game, playable on **laptop and phone**, **landscape only**. A laptop and a phone can play each other.
- Ship first on itch.io (free, pay-what-you-want, or paid). Static hosting (Cloudflare Pages or GitHub Pages) for free playtest links. **(Game Week)** The judging link is one of these.
- Later: Steam via a desktop wrapper (Electron or Tauri; $100 Steam Direct fee, recouped after $1,000 revenue). Phone app stores possible via a similar wrapper.
- Consoles are out of scope; they would need a port.
- **Engine choice stays Three.js** (#2). Unreal was considered and rejected: Unreal Engine 5 has no official browser export, Pixel Streaming needs a paid GPU server per player and adds lag that breaks rhythm judging, and web ports are unofficial.
- **(Game Week)** **No game server.** The game is static files, and all gameplay runs on the players' devices. The one exception is a tiny matchmaker and relay for multiplayer (#14). No accounts or online leaderboards in v1, so running cost stays $0 or close to it.

### 2. Engine and look
- **Three.js + TypeScript**, built into static files.
- **(Game Week)** **Stylized-realistic visuals** (see #10): real proportions, uniform detail, real instrument shapes, physically based materials (fabric, metal, shiny brass) and stadium lighting with shadows. Not photoreal faces or skin.
- **(Game Week)** **Two levels of detail:** the **two human players** use the most detailed marcher model, always clearly defined. **Computer marchers** use a lighter version of the same design (same uniform and colors, fewer details), drawn as one repeated (instanced) model. Resolution and frame rate are capped on phones.
- Small downloads: compressed models and audio.
- The game reads *actions*, never raw keys, so keyboard, touch and phone motion all map onto the same actions (see #8). Remote players are fed in as a stream of their positions, not their actions (#14).
- Only ship assets and music we made, arranged or generated ourselves (#9, #10). No "Halo" in the name or marketing. No real team or school logos (trademarks); field logos are invented.
- **Top-right corner: full-field top-down view** (not realistic, more fun). Both players are highlighted on it in their own colors. The same top-down drawing code powers the drill preview tool. Its detail can shrink at higher levels.

### 3. Timing
- **The music is the master clock** (the browser's Web Audio clock). Visuals draw from the song position, so frame rate never affects judging.
- **(Game Week)** In multiplayer, **each device plays its own copy of the music** and judges its own player against it. The host schedules a shared start moment (#14). Being a few milliseconds apart only shifts how the other player looks on your screen, never anyone's score.
- **Everything is written and shown in counts.** The player only ever sees counts. A tempo map module converts counts to seconds, including tempo changes, rit./accel. and meter changes.
- **(Game Week)** **Tempo maps come from the generated tracks.** Because AI-generated audio won't follow a grid exactly, each finished track is beat-tracked (automatic detection, then checked by ear) and its beat times become the show's tempo map. Drill pages and big moments are then placed on that map.
- **For now, every move and every hold is 8 counts.**
- **Count-off:** "5 … 6 … 5, 6, 7, 8" (the first two on every other beat).
- **The on-screen counter runs in fours, like the music** (1 2 3 4 1 2 3 4), with a stronger click on each 1.
- Taps and key presses are timestamped when they happen, not on the next frame.
- **Calibration screen** ("tap along with the clicks"), saved per device. If the delay is very large (Bluetooth), recommend wired headphones. In multiplayer, each player calibrates their own device.
- No audio library; the browser's built-in audio is enough.

**Beat windows** (starting values, tune in playtests):

| Gap from the count | Grade |
|---|---|
| ≤ ~45 ms | Perfect |
| ≤ ~90 ms | Good |
| ≤ ~150 ms | Rushing (early) / Dragging (late) |
| beyond | Miss |

- Early and late are called out as **rushing** and **dragging**.
- Windows are the **same at every level**. Difficulty comes from the music and drill, not tighter windows.
- Windows are fixed in milliseconds, so both very slow and very fast tempos are naturally hard.

**Dot accuracy** is shown to the player in **inches** from the dot, or **feet** when far off. Grade bands are TBD (for example: Perfect within ~6 in, Good within ~12 in).

### 4. Show file
- One JSON file per show: tempo map, performers, and a list of sets.
- **Positions are stored in 8-to-5 steps**, in quarter steps (0.25) only.
  - Side to side: steps from the 50 yard line (side 1 negative, side 2 positive; yard lines every 8 steps).
  - Front to back: steps from the front sideline.
  - Inches are for grading and display only (1 step = 22.5 in).
- **Each set (page):** page number, counts, every performer's dot and **facing**, and forms (which performers make up each line, arc or block).
- **(Game Week)** **Player slots:** each show marks two performers as `player1` and `player2`, always neighbors in the same form for most of the show, so their drift affects each other's FORM. Any slot not taken by a human is a computer marcher (solo play still works).
- **Facing per page:** *front* (horn to the box; the default and most of the show; backward marching when the dot is behind you), *toward the dot* (turn and march forward), or *backfield*. Each level sets how much of the show is backward marching and turned facings. Turns happen automatically on the first count.
- **A hold is its own page** with the same dots ("move 8, hold 8" is two pages). On a hold, the player stands still; pressing a key on a hold is a fault. No mark time. Moves are straight lines with equal steps.
- **Big musical moments** (hits, climaxes, the closer) are marked in the show file; they drive crowd cheers and General Effect points (see #7, #11). **(Game Week)** They're marked against the generated track's tempo map.
- **(Game Week)** **Hazards** (judge paths, staff runs) are listed per show with their timing, and placed from the run's seed so both devices see the same hazards at the same moment.
- **Step size is calculated**, not stored, and shown drill-sheet style ("8 to 5", "6 to 5"). 8-to-5 is home base: Level 1 is pure 8-to-5, bigger steps come at higher levels, and the show checker flags moves no human could make.
- **Drill-sheet language translator** both ways: numbers to "2 steps outside side 1 40, 3 steps behind front hash" and back. Used by the HUD and by staff voice lines.
- **Performers:** ID, fake name, section, instrument. **Brass only** for now; guard and percussion later.
- **(Game Week)** **Band size: 20–30 marchers per show**, including the two players. Enough to read as a band, light enough for phones, and every marcher stays meaningful to FORM.
- **Field:** college hashes only for now. Other field types later. Higher levels make the field busier (extra hash marks, yard numbers, a center logo). Logos are invented, never real teams.

### 5. Writing shows
- Shows are written in **drill-sheet text** (for example `T3, page 14, 8 counts: 2 outside S1 40, 3 behind FH`), and the translator turns it into the JSON show file.
- **The AI assistant writes the drill**, original and in the style of real drill, never copying a specific corps' show.
- A **drill style guide** (`docs/drill-style-guide.md`, to be written with Taylor) holds what good drill looks like at each level. Any session that writes drill reads it first.
- **(Game Week)** **Music first, then drill:** the track is generated and beat-mapped before its drill is written, so pages land on real phrases and hits.
- An automatic **show checker** validates every show: quarter steps only, step size within the level's limit, no marchers passing through each other, minimum spacing, counts that match the music. **(Game Week)** It also checks that both player slots exist and stay neighbors for most of the show.
- A **top-down preview** (director's view, like Pyware pages) for flipping through pages and reviewing drill.
- A visual drill editor is deferred until it's needed.

### 6. Other marchers
- Computer-controlled marchers follow the show file, with positions calculated from the song clock (no physics engine).
- **Everyone marches in step with the player:** one step per count, starting on the count, with the same step motion. When the player marches perfectly, the whole band moves together.
- **They have "temperature":** their mistakes are in *where* they go, not *when*. Each marcher has a tendency (cuts corners or bows out, marches big or small) **plus fresh randomness every rep**. Two reps of the same pages play out differently, so the player has to adapt.
- **(Game Week)** In multiplayer, the host picks the run's random seed and shares it, so **both players see the same band drift**. The match is fair: both are scored against the same luck.
- Temperature rises with the level.
- Errors are smooth and human: they build through a move and are mostly corrected near the dot. No teleporting, jitter or impossible steps.
- Each run records its random seed, so odd runs can be replayed for debugging (and possibly ghost runs later).
- Collisions with marchers, staff, judges, **or the other player**: stumble, points lost, director reacts.
- **Staff** run through at random, yelling at you or other marchers, to distract and get in the way. **(Game Week: in scope.)**
- **Judges (green shirts)** walk slowly through the band and can stand between you and your dot, blocking your view; you march around them. **(Game Week: in scope.)**
- Deferred to v2: neighbors reacting to the player (errors rippling down the line).
- Feature idea: **rehearsal mode** ("run pages 12–16 again").

### 7. Scoring
- **Two player scores, always visible: DOT and FORM.** The player decides moment to moment which to favor; when neighbors drift, the clean form no longer runs through the dot, so both can't be perfect.
  - **DOT:** inches from your dot on each page's arrival count, plus a light mid-move check (no wandering and snapping in at the end).
  - **FORM:** how well your position fits the form *as the band actually is*, **including the other human player**.
- **Timing** (how well your nudges land on the beat), **horns**, and **collisions** also count toward the player's result. With no nudges, DOT and FORM decide alone.
- **(Game Week)** **Not steering must not win.** The known Level 1 issue (#13) is fixed before anything else: more band drift and less pull, so a player who never steers scores clearly worse than one who does.
- **Improved FORM judging (confirmed Nov 2; required before multiplayer competition):** replace the step-0 four-neighbor average with shape-aware fitting and a separate interval error. Fit the form from the other members' **actual**, count-aligned positions, excluding the player being judged so they cannot move their own reference. Use line alignment and even intervals for lines; smooth fitted curvature and along-arc spacing for arcs; rank/file alignment and intervals for blocks. During transitions, blend the outgoing and incoming form references continuously instead of abruptly changing the scoring target.
  - Both human players are members of the fit and each other's immediate-neighbor spacing checks. A player on their written dot can lose FORM when their partner or band drifts, and can improve FORM by following the live shape at some DOT cost.
  - Weight immediate neighbors more heavily, but bound any one member's influence so one wildly misplaced marcher cannot drag the entire reference across the field. Use the same rules for humans and computer marchers. Keep fitting local and driven by each device's music clock; never wait for a network response to judge a beat.
  - Validate translated/rotated lines, curved arcs, rank/file distortions, uneven intervals, partner-only drift, self-exclusion, continuity through transitions, and both player slots symmetrically. Tune the shape/spacing weights in playtests; do not hide fixed weighting decisions in rendering code.
  - **Implementation boundary:** step 1 tunes steering against the existing local-neighbor FORM approximation. The full fitter and spacing caption above are a required follow-up before step 4; they are not claimed as complete by the step-1 build.
- **What "clean" means** (matches real drill charts): even spacing (intervals), straight lines, blocks with aligned ranks and files, smooth arcs, wedge rows parallel and evenly spaced. Measured by fitting the ideal shape to everyone's actual positions, then scoring deviation and spacing evenness.
- **The band's performance is partly luck:** its baseline comes from the computer marchers' temperature that run. The final result combines **your DOT and FORM scores** with **the band's rolled performance**.
- Tune the mix so a strong run passes almost every time, but the band can still have an off night.
- **Results screen** styled like a recap sheet: Visual (dot, form, spacing), Music (timing), General Effect (big moments, crowd). 100-point scale with decimals.
- **(Game Week)** **Head to head:** the recap shows both players side by side, caption by caption, with a winner banner. The band's rolled performance is the same for both (shared seed), so it never decides the winner. Ties go to the better DOT score.
- **(Game Week)** **During the show:** both players' running scores sit on the HUD, with a small "ahead / behind" indicator so position is always clear.

### 8. Controls
- **You march automatically**, every count of a move page, at the right step size, toward your dot. Feet alternate automatically.
- **(Game Week)** **Visible footwork:** every marcher's legs animate on the counts, **left foot on odd counts, right foot on even counts**, like real marching. Forward is a heel-to-toe roll; backward marching is up on the toes. Holds stand still with feet together. Purely visual; controls don't change.
- **The player only steers left / right, on the beat.** Each nudge is judged against the nearest count. An off-beat nudge (miss) is wasted. A rushed nudge applies to that count's step; a dragged one applies to the next.
- **Keyboard:** **← / →** nudge, **Space** horns up / down (later), **Tab** dot book, **Esc** pause (in multiplayer: opens the menu, the show keeps running).
- **Phone:** **turn the phone like a steering wheel to steer** (gradual, with a small dead zone), read on each beat. Swipe up / down for horns. A tap-zones fallback (left / right of the screen, on the beat) is available as a setting. The start screen asks for motion permission (needed on iPhone). **(Game Week: in scope, since phones join multiplayer.)**
- **(Game Week)** **Phones keep the screen awake** during a show (Wake Lock), since a locked screen drops the connection.
- **Steering model:** your path = *direction to your dot + an offset*. Each page starts with zero offset (heading snaps to the dot, which also re-centers phone tilt). ← / → nudge the offset a few degrees, **relative to your path, not the screen** (works the same marching backward or sliding). Movement is blended into smooth curves, never zig-zags.
- **Pull toward the dot** set per level: strong on Level 1 (forgiving), none on the hardest levels. Never so strong that you can't hold a line off your dot. Nudge size and pull strength are tuning settings.
- **Camera swings around automatically** when you're moving mostly backward, so you can see your dot.
- **Dynamics are automatic** for now (they follow the music). Manual dynamics may come back later as a harder-level skill.

### 9. Music
- **Public domain compositions only.** Never use a corps' arrangement or a modern edited edition; work from old public-domain scores (for example, IMSLP).
- Prefer pieces that are **public domain worldwide** (composer died 1955 or earlier and published 1930 or earlier). Avoid US-only public domain (Stravinsky, Sibelius, Glière).
- Candidate list: `docs/pd-repertoire.md` (DCI-used pieces sorted by public-domain status). Dates there still need checking.
- **(Game Week)** **AI-generated tracks in a drum corps style.** Confirm with the instructor that this counts (#0).
  - **Pipeline:** pick the piece → the AI assistant writes a short arrangement from the public-domain score (melody, form, tempo, where the hits and climaxes go) → render it as a simple demo → feed the demo and a text style prompt ("drum and bugle corps, full brass, battery percussion, outdoor stadium") to the AI music tool → pick the best take → beat-track it into a tempo map (#3).
  - **Style comes from text prompts and our own demo, never from corps recordings.** Corps videos are fine for *us* to watch and learn the sound, but we don't upload them to the generator: the arrangements are the corps' copyright, many tools' terms forbid uploading audio you don't own, and the output could echo the original chart.
  - **Check the tool's terms** for commercial use before picking it, so the tracks can ship if the game is sold later.
  - **Keep the prompts, demos and chosen takes** in the repo so a track can be regenerated or traced.
- **(Game Week)** **Shows are short:** about 1.5–2.5 minutes each, so a match is quick and replayable.
- The click track and count-off stay synthesized in code (#3).
- Who arranges: the AI assistant drafts the arrangements and prompts; Taylor reviews and picks takes.

### 10. Art

Every screen follows it. Pick one realism level and apply it everywhere; half-realistic people look worse than consistent stylized ones.
- **(Game Week)** **Marcher detail:** a shako with plume and chin strap, a jacket with cross straps or a sash, bibbers, gauntlets, gloves and marching shoes. Real instrument shapes (trumpet, mellophone, baritone, tuba) in polished brass. Uniform colors are a setting.
- **(Game Week)** **Hero vs. band:** the two players get the full-detail model and are always clearly defined: distinct plume color, a subtle outline or rim light, and a marker on their dot. Computer marchers use a lighter version of the same model (#2).
- **(Game Week)** **How the hero model gets made** is decided with the style sheet: either built in code at higher detail, or AI-generated and rigged (if the instructor allows it, #0). Either way, no asset-store or library characters.
- **(Game Week)** **A realistic field:** turf with mowing stripes, painted yard numbers, hashes and sidelines, and an invented center logo.
- **(Game Week)** **A stadium around the field:** stands, lights and a simple crowd (instanced shapes), so the field feels like a place.
- **Visible drum major on a podium (Taylor, Nov 2):** the conductor is a drum major, visibly depicted on a raised podium at the front sideline. The player must be able to see the drum major's figure/image in the game, not just hear a voice or see a label. Conducting follows the same music clock and meter as the count-off and show; it never becomes a separate timing authority. The walking band director/staff are separate characters. Create the visual for this game, with no stock assets or real insignia. Choose a detailed code-built/rigged figure or an original generated image/animated sprite during the step-2 style pass, and frame it clearly from the playing camera (an inset may preserve visibility when the camera swings backward).
- The marcher lives in one module so a better model can replace it later without touching the rest of the game.

### 11. Crowd and director audio
- **Crowd:** volume and mood follow your recent pages, swelling and souring gradually. **Big cheers only at the show's marked big moments, and only if you're scoring well.** Boos when things fall apart.
- **Band director:** yells at you when you mess up, and **also at random at other marchers by their fake names**, so the band feels alive. Your mistakes take priority. Lines are picked by what went wrong (rushing, dragging, dot, form, collision, great page) and have a cooldown. In multiplayer, each player hears the lines about themselves.
- **Staff:** random, yelling at you or others.
- **(Game Week)** **Sources:** crowd sounds **generated by us** (synthesized noise shaped into cheers and boos, AI-generated, or recorded by Taylor and friends), not free libraries. **Voices recorded by Taylor and friends.** Text-to-speech is a fallback only if recording doesn't happen in time.
- **Every voice line also appears as a text bubble** (muted phones, hearing access, loud passages).
- **Music always wins:** crowd and voices duck under the music.
- Taylor writes the lines in a list grouped by event.

### 12. Backend
- **(Game Week)** **No game server.** Progress, best scores, settings and calibration are saved on the player's device (browser storage).
- The only online pieces are the matchmaker and relay for multiplayer (#14). They pass messages between players; they don't run the game or store anything.
- Tradeoff accepted: clearing browser data wipes progress, and progress doesn't sync between devices.
- Cloud saves and leaderboards may come later as a small add-on.

### 13. First playable slice (built)
**The question it answers:** is steering on the beat toward your dot, while the line drifts around you, fun?

**Built (repo `mb-game`):**
- Laptop, keyboard only (← / →).
- Level 1: 10 pages, all 8 counts (moves and holds), steady 112 BPM, with a backward-march page.
- 12 brass marchers: line → arc → hold → backward → block → forward → hold → line → hold.
- Click track with the 5-6-5678 count-off; counter in fours.
- Tempo map, calibration screen, beat judging, auto-march with steering and pull, band marching in step with the player, drifting marchers, DOT and FORM scores, corner map, camera with backward swing, recap screen.
- Drill-sheet translator and show checker, with automated tests.

**Step 1 tuning (Nov 2):** idle marching no longer follows a mathematically perfect path. Each run/page has a small seeded spatial heading tendency (not a timing error); on-beat steering counters it. Heading still resets each page. Dot pull is reduced from 0.18 to 0.035; nudge size is 0.16 radians with bounded accumulated heading and 0.96 retention. Computer drift includes a shared smooth offset plus individual errors, advances only during moves and settles to a smaller residual at arrivals. Holds keep feet planted. The constants live together in `STEERING` for playtest tuning.

**Scoring for this playtest:** DOT retains arrival-heavy checking (75% arrivals / 25% mid-move). DOT and FORM share the visual result equally; timing modifies that visual result by up to 15%, rather than awarding free points for never steering. With no nudges, only visual accuracy and the 5% shared band result determine the total. Hold faults still subtract points. Automated fixed-seed comparisons require corrective on-beat steering to beat idle by at least 3 points on every tested seed and by over 10 points at the median. This proves a useful correction is reachable, not that first-time-player difficulty is settled; Taylor's playtest is the next gate.

### 14. Multiplayer (Game Week)
- **Confirmed scope (Taylor, Nov 2): exactly two human players for now.** Both occupy adjacent slots in the same form for most pages. Neither is an independent lane or a visual ghost: each player's actual displacement affects the other's FORM integrity. More than two players remains out of scope.
- **Two players, online, peer-to-peer.** Laptop vs laptop, phone vs phone, or mixed.
- **Joining:** one player taps **Host** and gets a short room code plus a share link; the other enters the code or opens the link. Both land in a lobby showing who's there and which show is picked.
- **How devices find each other:** WebRTC. A tiny **matchmaker** (signaling, on a free tier such as Cloudflare Workers or PeerJS) passes a few setup messages, then steps out. A **relay** (TURN) carries traffic when a direct connection is blocked, which is common on cellular and school Wi-Fi. Built in from day one, not added later.
- **What's sent:** the run's seed and show choice (once), then each player's position and heading several times per beat, and score updates. A few hundred bytes a second.
- **Starting together:** the host measures the round-trip delay, then sends "start the count-off at this exact moment." Each device plays its own music from there.
- **Each device is the judge of its own player.** Remote players are drawn from their received positions, smoothed between updates. Lag shifts where your friend appears, never anyone's score.
- **Shared luck:** same seed on both devices, so the same band drift and the same hazards.
- **Drops:** if the connection drops or a player backgrounds the app, the other player sees "Your friend dropped." Their marcher is taken over by the computer and the show finishes. The recap marks the dropped player as incomplete.
- **Solo play still works** (both player slots can be computer marchers except yours), useful for practice and for judges playing alone.
- **Testing:** two browser windows on one machine, then laptop + phone on Wi-Fi, then phone on cellular.
